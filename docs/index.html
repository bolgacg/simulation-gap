Before anything else

Five things to know

  1. deeptangle finds worms in microscopy footage where they overlap and tangle. It was published by this group in 2023 and its code is open.
  2. It never saw a real worm in training. Every training frame came from a simulator written alongside it, which draws worms and moves them according to physics.
  3. That is the idea the DREAM project is built on: train on synthetic data where the answer is known by construction, then close the distance to real experiments.
  4. The simulator has settings, and someone chose them. This page asks which of those choices the result actually depends on, by changing one at a time, retraining, and scoring against real footage a human labelled. Four settings are swept here, not all of them, and which four is stated in act two.
  5. Then the real question: can you pick good settings without any labels at all, just by making the synthetic images look statistically like the real ones? That is the part of the project that would matter in a laboratory, and it is the last act.
The loop this page measures
Terms
simulator setting One of the numbers deciding what a synthetic worm looks like and how it moves. The training script exposes six flags that reach the simulator, covering how many worms and how big the frame is. Everything about the worm itself is a literal inside two source files; this study lifted 27 of those into a configuration object, which is a count of what was lifted rather than a census of what exists.
real score How well a trained model finds the worms a human marked in real footage. Higher is better. It is a recall: of the worms a human marked, the share the model found. A worm counts as found when the average distance from the drawn centreline to the predicted one, along the drawn one, is under three pixels. It is an average and not a worst case, so a prediction can be well off at the head and still pass if the rest of the body is close.
precision The other half of the same picture: of everything the model claimed was a worm, the share that matched something a human marked. It depends on the confidence threshold the detector is run at, which here is the repository's own default of 0.5, so it is a property of the model at that setting rather than of the model. On this evaluation set it is also a floor rather than a rate, because the hand labels turn out to sit in the middle of each frame while detections are counted across all of it. The bottom of this page measures that and says what it costs.
image statistics Numbers describing what a frame looks like, computed the same way on synthetic and real footage, using no labels.
the gap The distance between doing well on synthetic frames and doing well on real ones. Closing it without labels is the project's stated aim.
Their words

The question is the fellowship's own

The advertisement states the method and its hard part in the same breath, and this page takes the hard part literally.

The aim is to train models on synthetic data generated by biophysical simulations by developing simulations that can be automatically tuned to be realistic enough that the trained models generalize to real experimental data. [...] A central challenge is to automatically tune simulations so that synthetic datasets match the variability, noise, and structure of real, unlabelled biological datasets. University of Copenhagen, PhD fellowship 165870, Simulation-Supervised Machine Learning for Biology, read 14 September 2026

The detector, the simulator and the labelled real footage are all this group's own published work. That choice was deliberate. A study built on some other laboratory's system would need an argument that its answer carries over; this one either applies to the fellowship's first month or it is wrong, and there is no translation step in between where the claim could quietly weaken.

Act two

One setting at a time

Why this matters A simulator has many ways to be wrong and a laboratory cannot tune all of them, so knowing which ones the result actually moves with is what decides where the effort goes. Four settings are swept here, chosen because each changes something physically different about the worm or the image. That is a sample of the simulator, not a survey of it, and nothing here speaks for the settings that were left alone.

Each point on the chart is one model, trained from scratch with a single simulator setting moved away from the value the authors chose, and scored on the same real footage as every other point. Pick a setting to see what moving it does.

Act three

Can you choose the settings without labels?

Why this matters In a laboratory the labels do not exist. If settings can only be chosen by scoring against labelled data, the method needs the thing it was meant to avoid.

Every configuration is ranked twice. Once by how well its model does on real labelled footage, which is the answer. Once by how closely its synthetic frames resemble the real ones statistically, which uses no labels at all. If the second ranking tracks the first, the tuning loop the fellowship proposes has something to stand on.

How it was built

Method, limits and sources

What was changed, and what was not

The published repository is used unmodified. The 27 simulator settings live as literals inside two of its files, so a small layer replaces those two functions at run time with configured versions and leaves the clone untouched. With no configuration supplied, the training script is exactly the repository's own. That is deliberate: a study of someone's simulator that first rewrites their simulator is not a study of their simulator.

Model card

Every model on this page was trained here from scratch. None of them is a model anyone should use, and that is deliberate: they are probes of a simulator, trained briefly and identically so that the only thing separating them is the setting under test.

Limits, stated before you find them

One species, one imaging setup, one laboratory's footage. Nothing here says which settings matter for a different organism or a different microscope, and the honest expectation is that the answer differs.

Training runs here are shorter than the published one, so every score is lower than the paper's and only the comparisons between them mean anything. The baseline in act one is there precisely so the reader can see that offset rather than guess at it.
